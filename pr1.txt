MNN2 can treat MNN1 as the deterministic algorithmic reasoning engine and add the interpretive layer: natural language, time series, contextual memory, ranking, signposting, rules, Q&A, nuances, and knowledge application—while preserving data-readable traces.

MNN2 — Prolog AI Agent Program Requirements

1. Project Name

MNN2 — Interpretive Manual Neuronet

2. Purpose

Build an SWI-Prolog interpretive intelligence and chatbot system on top of MNN1.

MNN1 investigates deterministic algorithm discovery, neuroalgorithm composition, optimisation and multivariate reasoning.

MNN2 adds:

* natural-language interpretation;
* conversational context;
* time-series reasoning;
* temporal memory;
* rule interpretation;
* question answering;
* deterministic relevance ranking;
* signposting;
* nuance handling;
* contextual reinterpretation;
* knowledge application;
* multi-step analysis;
* response planning;
* human-readable explanations;
* importable user knowledge.

MNN2 should use MNN1 as its principal algorithmic reasoning substrate rather than duplicate it.

⸻

3. Architectural Principle

Use:

MNN1
=
algorithmic intelligence
MNN2
=
interpretive intelligence

Therefore:

Language / Documents / Events
            ↓
     MNN2 Interpretation
            ↓
    Canonical Knowledge
            ↓
       Context + Time
            ↓
      Goal / Question
            ↓
     Relevance Selection
            ↓
          MNN1
            ↓
   Algorithmic Reasoning
            ↓
    MNN2 Interpretation
            ↓
     Planned Response
            ↓
      Natural Language

Do not make the language interface itself the reasoning engine.

⸻

4. Research Question

Investigate:

Can deterministic Manual Neuronets combine language interpretation, temporal context, explicit rules and algorithmic reasoning to provide useful conversational reasoning without making the underlying reasoning dependent on statistical neural weights?

Secondary questions:

1. Can natural language be mapped to reusable symbolic structures?
2. Can conversation history be represented as an explicit time series?
3. Can relevant historical information be selected deterministically?
4. Can ranking replace or supplement statistical attention for contextual retrieval?
5. Can signposts identify how statements relate to previous statements?
6. Can rules expressed in language be converted into executable MNN structures?
7. Can MNN1 algorithms be selected from ordinary questions?
8. Can multiple interpretations be maintained where language is ambiguous?
9. Can answers remain grounded in the source data?
10. Can reasoning and linguistic presentation remain separate?
11. Can knowledge from multiple sources be applied compositionally?
12. Can the system explain why particular information was considered relevant?

⸻

7. Canonical Knowledge Representation

Translate linguistic variations into common structures.

Example:

John owns an apple.
The apple belongs to John.
John possesses the apple.

may canonicalise to:

owns(john, apple).

Likewise:

Alice arrived before Bob.
Bob arrived after Alice.

may share:

before(arrival(alice), arrival(bob)).

Store both:

source_text(...)
canonical_form(...)

so natural-language provenance is not lost.

⸻

8. Sentence Interpretation

Support common sentence functions including:

* assertion;
* question;
* command;
* rule;
* definition;
* correction;
* qualification;
* comparison;
* preference;
* hypothetical;
* condition;
* consequence;
* explanation;
* example;
* counterexample;
* temporal statement;
* causal statement;
* uncertainty statement.

Example:

If a customer buys three products, give them free delivery.

Interpret approximately as:

rule(
    free_delivery(Customer),
    [
        customer(Customer),
        product_count(Customer, Count),
        Count >= 3
    ]
).

Retain the original sentence.

⸻

9. Interpretation Alternatives

Do not force premature interpretation where ambiguity exists.

Represent:

interpretations(
    Input,
    [
        interpretation(I1, Evidence1),
        interpretation(I2, Evidence2)
    ]
).

Subsequent context may eliminate alternatives.

Where ambiguity materially affects the answer and cannot be resolved, ask the user.

⸻

10. Conversational Time Series

Conversation must not be represented merely as a flat transcript.

Store events:

event(
    EventId,
    Time,
    Speaker,
    Type,
    CanonicalContent,
    SourceText
).

Example:

t1: John works at A.
t2: John leaves A.
t3: John joins B.

Current query:

Where does John work?

MNN2 should reason over the temporal sequence rather than return the oldest matching fact.

12. State Evolution

Represent changing properties as state transitions.

Example:

Monday: box is red.
Tuesday: box is painted blue.

Do not conclude that its current state is both red and blue unless the domain permits that interpretation.

Represent approximately:

state(box, colour, red, T1).
transition(box, colour, red, blue, T2).
state(box, colour, blue, T2).

17. Signposting

Signposting identifies the functional relationship of a new statement to existing material.

Examples:

however
therefore
for example
in contrast
because
similarly
first
second
more importantly
on the other hand
in summary

Represent abstractly:

signpost(Statement, contrast, Previous).
signpost(Statement, evidence_for, Claim).
signpost(Statement, example_of, Rule).
signpost(Statement, consequence_of, Cause).

Also infer structural signposts when sufficiently clear from explicit relationships.

⸻

18. Signposting as Reasoning Structure

Signposts should affect interpretation.

Example:

Most birds in this example fly.
However, Penguin is a penguin.

however signals that the second statement may qualify or contrast with the preceding generalisation.

Do not treat discourse markers merely as decorative words.

⸻

19. Rules

MNN2 should support natural-language rules.

Example:

If X owns Y and Y is red, then X owns something red.

Canonical representation:

rule(
    owns_red_object(X),
    [
        owns(X,Y),
        colour(Y,red)
    ]
).

Rules may be sent to MNN1 for compilation into neuroalgorithms.

⸻

20. Rule Sources

Track whether a rule came from:

built-in system
user
document
dataset
S2A
AIOC
derived rule
imported algorithm

Never silently confuse user-provided rules with universally established facts.

21. Q&A Interpretation

Classify questions by required operation.

Examples:

Who...?       → entity retrieval
What...?      → property/definition
When...?      → temporal reasoning
Where...?     → spatial/location reasoning
Why...?       → causal/explanatory trace
How...?       → procedure/algorithm
Which...?     → selection/comparison
What changed? → temporal difference
What if...?   → hypothetical reasoning

Question classification should help route the query to appropriate neuroalgorithms.

⸻

22. Question Decomposition

Complex questions should be decomposable.

Example:

Which customer bought the most products last month
and did they receive free delivery?

may become:

Q1: customers last month
Q2: product counts
Q3: maximum customer
Q4: free-delivery status
Q5: combine answer

Each subquestion can invoke MNN1 independently.

⸻

23. Deterministic Relevance Ranking

Rank candidate information using explicit criteria.

Potential criteria:

* exact entity match;
* exact predicate match;
* question relevance;
* temporal relevance;
* current-state relevance;
* topic relevance;
* causal relevance;
* rule dependency;
* source relationship;
* specificity;
* recency where appropriate;
* signpost relationship.

The ranking procedure must be inspectable.

⸻

24. Ranking Is Not Truth

High relevance does not establish truth.

Maintain the distinction:

ranking
=
what should be examined first

versus:

reasoning
=
what follows from the selected data/rules

⸻

25. Deterministic Attention

MNN2 attention should combine indexes and ranking.

Conceptually:

Question
   ↓
entities + predicates + time + goal
   ↓
parallel indexes
   ↓
candidate intersection/union
   ↓
explicit ranking
   ↓
small relevant working set
   ↓
MNN1

Record why each selected item was considered relevant.

⸻

26. Nuance Representation

Represent modifiers that materially affect meaning.

Examples:

always
usually
sometimes
only
except
probably
possibly
must
may
should
apparently
reportedly
approximately
exactly
nearly

Do not strip them during canonicalisation.

Represent their semantic effect explicitly where supported.

Example:

modal(may, proposition(...)).

or:

qualified(
    proposition(...),
    approximately
).

⸻

27. Negation

Distinguish:

X is not red.
It is unknown whether X is red.
No evidence says X is red.

Do not collapse absence of knowledge into explicit negation.

⸻

28. Contradictions

When conflicting propositions occur:

t1: A is red.
t2: A is blue.

first determine whether:

* the state changed;
* sources disagree;
* contexts differ;
* a hypothetical is involved;
* one statement corrects another.

Only report a contradiction after contextual/temporal resolution fails.

⸻

29. Corrections

Recognise:

Actually...
I meant...
Correction...
No, X rather than Y.

Represent correction relations explicitly.

Do not simply delete history.

Example:

supersedes(NewEvent, OldEvent).

This preserves the conversational time series.

⸻

30. Knowledge Application

MNN2 should support applying one piece of knowledge to another.

Conceptually:

Knowledge A
      +
Knowledge B
      ↓
applicable relation/algorithm?
      ↓
MNN1
      ↓
Knowledge C

This can be repeated recursively.

However, avoid unrestricted combinatorial “everything × everything” evaluation.

Use relevance indexes, types, preconditions and goals to constrain candidate combinations.

⸻

31. Knowledge-to-Knowledge Matching

Each canonical proposition should expose:

predicate
arguments
types
relations

context
dependencies

Use these properties to determine whether another rule, fact or neuroalgorithm can consume it.

33. MNN1 Invocation

Define an interface approximately equivalent to:

mnn1_reason(
    Goal,
    Facts,
    Rules,
    Context,
    Result,
    Trace
).

MNN2 prepares the symbolic inputs.

MNN1 performs algorithmic reasoning.

MNN2 then converts the result and trace into appropriate conversational output.

34. Human-Readable Trace

As with MNN1, primary traces must be expressed through data.

Example:

Question:
What colour is John's fruit?
Relevant information:
John owns the apple.
The apple is red.
Reasoning:
The object owned by John is the apple.
The apple is red.
Answer:
John's fruit is red.

Do not normally show:

N7 → N21 → R4 → N19

Such details belong in debug mode.

⸻

35. Temporal Explanation

Example:

Monday:
John worked at A.
Wednesday:
John left A.
Friday:
John joined B.
Question:
Where does John currently work?

Trace:

John initially worked at A.
He later left A.
He subsequently joined B.
The later employment state supersedes the earlier one.
Therefore:
John currently works at B.

36. Response Planning

Do not directly stringify the first successful Prolog result.

Create a response plan.

Example:

response_plan([
    answer(MainAnswer),
    explanation(Reasoning),
    qualification(Qualifier)
]).

Possible sections:

* direct answer;
* explanation;
* evidence;
* uncertainty;
* alternative interpretation;
* next useful detail.

Only include sections useful for the query.

⸻

37. Response Modes

Support:

concise
normal
detailed
technical
debug

concise may return primarily the answer.

normal gives useful reasoning.

detailed gives fuller data-level reasoning.

technical may discuss algorithms.

debug may expose Prolog/MNN internals.

38. Natural-Language Generation

Generate responses from structured results.

Separate:

 ↓

 ↓

 ↓

55. AIOC in MNN2

AIOC should optimise recurring interpretive pipelines.

Example:

pronoun resolution
→ temporal resolution
→ ownership lookup
→ property lookup

If this composition repeatedly solves a class of questions, investigate whether it can become a macro-neuroalgorithm.

Example:

"current property of previously referenced owned object"

The resulting macro must remain general and testable.

Users may explicitly teach algorithms.

 ↓

 ↓

57. Algorithm Retrieval From Questions

Question:

How much is a $100 product after a $20 discount?

Interpretation:

original_price(100).
discount(20).
goal(final_price).

Retrieve:

sale-price algorithm

MNN1 executes it.

MNN2 responds:

The final price is $80 because the $20 discount is subtracted from the $100 original price.

⸻

58. Knowledge Combination Control

Avoid combinatorial explosion.

Do not execute:

every fact × every rule × every algorithm

Instead:

goal
 ↓
required output type
 ↓
relevant predicates
 ↓
relevant entities/time/context
 ↓
candidate algorithms
 ↓
precondition check
 ↓
execute

This is a major role of MNN2’s deterministic attention system.

⸻

72. Example: Conversational Correction

Conversation:

User:
Alice works for Alpha.
User:
Alice moved to Beta last month.
User:
Actually, she moved to Gamma, not Beta.
User:
Where does Alice work?

Expected:

Alice works for Gamma.
You first said she worked for Alpha, then that she moved to Beta. You subsequently corrected the destination to Gamma, so Gamma is the current stated workplace.

73. Example: Rules + Q&A

Input:

A premium customer receives free shipping.
Alice is a premium customer.

Question:

Does Alice receive free shipping?

Output:

Yes. Alice is a premium customer, and premium customers receive free shipping.

⸻

74. Example: Signposting

Input:

Sales increased.
However, profit decreased because costs rose.

Question:

What happened?

Desired interpretation:

Sales increased, but that did not translate into higher profit. Profit decreased because rising costs offset the sales improvement.

The contrast and causal relationship should be represented explicitly.

⸻

75. Example: Temporal Reasoning

Input:

The project started in January.
Testing began in March.
The launch was delayed in April.
The product launched in June.

Question:

What happened between testing and launch?

Expected reasoning:

Testing began in March.
The launch was delayed in April.
The product eventually launched in June.
Therefore the recorded event between testing and launch was the April delay.

⸻

76. Example: Multi-Step Q&A

Knowledge:

John owns the apple.
The apple is in the kitchen.
The kitchen is upstairs.

Question:

Where is John's apple?

Possible answer:

John's apple is in the kitchen, which is upstairs.

Detailed trace:

John owns the apple.
The apple is in the kitchen.
The kitchen is upstairs.
Therefore John's apple is in the upstairs kitchen.

⸻

77. Example: Knowledge Application

Input:

A customer receives a 10% discount if they spend more than $100.
Alice spent $150.
The listed price is $200.

Question:

What should Alice pay?

MNN2:

interpret question
 ↓
retrieve discount rule
 ↓
establish Alice qualifies
 ↓
invoke MNN1 arithmetic algorithm
 ↓
calculate discount
 ↓
calculate final price

Output:

Alice should pay $180. Her $150 spending qualifies her for the 10% discount, and 10% off the $200 listed price is $20.

88. MNN1/MNN2 Separation

Maintain this boundary rigorously:

MNN1 asks:
"What algorithm transforms these inputs into the required result?"
MNN2 asks:
"What does the user mean, what information matters, when does it apply, what reasoning goal should be sent to MNN1, and how should the result be communicated?"

This separation is important experimentally and architecturally.

* external computer control;
* autonomous tool use;
* independent goal generation;
* multi-agent organisations;

92. Relationship Between MNN1 and MNN2

The intended repository relationship is:

MNN1
│
├── algorithm discovery
├── neuroalgorithms
├── deterministic attention primitives
├── algorithm composition
├── AIOC
├── optimisation
├── deterministic reasoning
└── multivariate generalisation
       │
       ▼
MNN2
│
├── natural-language interpretation
├── conversational time series
├── contextual memory
├── temporal reasoning
├── ranking
├── signposting
├── rules
├── Q&A
├── nuance
├── knowledge application
├── response planning
└── human-readable conversational explanation
       │
       ▼
MNN3
    agents / goals / tools / autonomous workflows

MNN2 should therefore demonstrate that a deterministic algorithmic MNN can be elevated from solving formal I/O reasoning problems into an interpretable conversational reasoning system without hiding the underlying computation behind an opaque response-generation process.

The essential invariant across MNN1 and MNN2 is:

The system may internally optimise algorithms aggressively, but its explanation of a particular reasoning episode must remain reconstructable from the actual human-readable data that entered, changed and left that computation.

This keeps the experimental progression clean: MNN1 = learn/combine/optimise algorithms; MNN2 = understand and reason conversationally with those algorithms; MNN3 = use that intelligence to pursue goals and operate tools.
